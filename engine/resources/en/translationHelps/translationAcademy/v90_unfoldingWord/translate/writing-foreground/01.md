### Description
When people tell a story, make an argument, or make a request, sometimes they need to tell their listeners that something that they have said or are about to say is the most important part of the story or argument or request. That type of information is called **foreground information**. It is the information that is most important for the listeners to understand or act on. People will often use a specific word or phrase to signal that this part is foreground information.
#### Examples
The bolded phrases in the examples below signal that what follows is foreground information. That is, what follows these phrases is the most important part of a story or argument or the specific request that someone has been leading up to by providing other information.

We’ve worked hard on this rose garden, fertilizing and watering the soil and pruning the branches. The bushes are beautiful and healthy, and we thought everything was fine. **But listen to this.** I just spotted two Japanese beetles on one of the bushes. We need to spray the bushes right away to kill those beetles and keep any more from coming and destroying the buds and blossoms.

Our community projects went pretty well last year, but I think they could have gone better. I’ve recognized that you are good at organizing people to work together. **So here is what I was hoping you would do.** Would you take a look at each of our projects and suggest ways that we could carry them out even better?

### Reasons This Is A translation Issue
* Languages have different ways of signaling what is foreground information. 
* You (the translator) need to be able to recognize which information in the Bible is foreground information so that you can signal it correctly in your language. 
* You will need to translate biblical stories, arguments, and requests in a way that enables your own readers to recognize what information in them is foreground information.
### Examples From the Bible
Ezra 5:7–17 presents a letter that Persian officials responsible for the area of Judea sent to the Persian emperor Darius. In that letter, those officials report that the Jews were rebuilding Yahweh’s temple and that a previous emperor, Cyrus, had given them permission. The Persian officials doubt this, so then they write:
> “**And now,** if it is good to the king, let a search be made in the house of the treasures of the king which is there in Babylon, if it is that a decree was set by Cyrus the king to build this house of God in Jerusalem. And let him send to us the will of the king concerning this.” (Ezra 5:17 ULT)

Most of this letter provides background information to help Darius understand why the officials are making their request. The expression “And now” signals that what follows is their specific request.

In 1 Corinthians 7:1–24, Paul addresses the Corinthians’ belief that it was more spiritual not to be married than to be married. He discusses the advantages and disadvantages of being married and of being single. He uses other situations in life as illustrations. He then says, 
> “**Brothers,** each one in that which he was called, let him remain with God in this” (1 Corinthians 7:24 ULT).

Paul uses the expression “Brothers” to signal that what follows is the essential point of this argument: if people are already married, they should remain married, since it is not more spiritual to be single.

In Joshua 7, the Israelites attack the small city of Ai, but they are defeated. Yahweh tells Joshua that this happened because one of the Israelites sinned against him by taking plunder from the city of Jericho, which he had forbidden. Yahweh identifies a man named Achan as the one who sinned. When Joshua confronts him, Achan confesses. He describes how he found a valuable garment and some silver and gold in Jericho and took them. He then says, 
> “**And behold,** they are hidden in the earth in the midst of my tent” (Joshua 7:21 ULT).

Achan uses the expression “And behold” to signal that what follows is the main point of the story that is his confession: he kept the plunder and hid it, showing that he knew that it was wrong to take it.
### Translation Strategies

To keep your translation clear and natural, you will need to study how people tell stories and make arguments and requests in your language. Observe how speakers of your language mark foreground information. You may need to write down some stories, arguments, and requests in order to study this. Observe what kinds of words, phrases, or other markers in your language signal that something is foreground information. Do these same things when you translate so that your translation is clear and natural and people can understand it easily.

(1) Use your language’s way of showing that certain information is foreground information.
(2) Reorder the information as appropriate, so that the foreground information comes at the most natural place for your language.

### Examples of Translation Strategies Applied
(1) Use your language’s way of showing that certain information is foreground information.

> “**And now,** if it is good to the king, let a search be made in the house of the treasures of the king which is there in Babylon,” (Ezra 5:17 ULT)

>> “**Accordingly,** if it is good to the king, let a search be made in the house of the treasures of the king which is there in Babylon,” (Ezra 5:17)
Or
>> “**We suggest that,** if it is good to the king, let a search be made in the house of the treasures of the king which is there in Babylon,” (Ezra 5:17)

> “**Brothers,** each one in that which he was called, let him remain with God in this” (1 Corinthians 7:24 ULT).

>> “**So then, brothers**, each one in that which he was called, let him remain with God in this” (1 Corinthians 7:24).
Or
>> “**Therefore, my fellow believers,** each one in that which he was called, let him remain with God in this” (1 Corinthians 7:24).

> “**And behold,** they are hidden in the earth in the midst of my tent” (Joshua 7:21 ULT).

>> “**So look,** they are hidden in the earth in the midst of my tent” (Joshua 7:21).
Or
>> “**And now you will find that** they are hidden in the earth in the midst of my tent” (Joshua 7:21).

(2) Reorder the information as appropriate, so that the foreground information comes at the most natural place for your language.

> “**And now,** if it is good to the king, let a search be made in the house of the treasures of the king which is there in Babylon, if it is that a decree was set by Cyrus the king to build this house of God in Jerusalem. And let him send to us the will of the king concerning this.” (Ezra 5:17 ULT).

>> “If it is good to the king, let a search be made in the house of the treasures of the king which is there in Babylon, if it is that a decree was set by Cyrus the king to build this house of God in Jerusalem. And let him send to us the will of the king concerning this. **This is our request.**” (Ezra 5:17 ULT).

In James 4:11–12, James first states his main point, signaled as foreground by the term “brothers,” and he then gives reasons for it. (In this instance, the term “brothers” follows the foreground information.)

> “Do not speak against one another, **brothers.** The one speaking against a brother or judging his brother speaks against the law and judges the law. But if you judge the law, you are not a doer of the law, but a judge. There is one lawgiver and judge, the one being able to save and to destroy. But who are you, the one judging your neighbor?” (James 4:11–12 ULT)

In some languages, it may be clearer or more natural for the main point to come after the reasons. It might also be clearer for the expression that signals foreground information to precede that information.

The one speaking against a brother or judging his brother speaks against the law and judges the law. But if you judge the law, you are not a doer of the law, but a judge. There is one lawgiver and judge, the one being able to save and to destroy. But who are you, the one judging your neighbor? **Brothers,** do not speak against one another. (James 4:11–12, reordered)
